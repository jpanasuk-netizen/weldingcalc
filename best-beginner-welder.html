Best beginner welder — pick the metal first

Everyone asks "which machine should I buy?" The better question is "what thickness will I weld?" — the machine follows from that.

Quick answer: A 140 A dual-process MIG/stick machine on 120 V covers most beginner work up to 3/16" steel — start with flux-core/MIG for clean steel, stick for rusty or outdoor work. Check the duty cycle at your working amperage before buying.

Step 1: your metal, not your budget

Write down the three heaviest things you'll actually weld. Trailer tongue? That's 3/16"–1/4". Exhaust patch? Thin pipe, 1/8" or less. Auto body? 20 ga sheet. This list decides everything — a machine sized for sheet metal is money wasted on trailer work and vice versa. Run each thickness through the thickness calculator to see the amps and rod it demands.

Step 3: three traps

Duty cycle marketing. A "140 amp" machine that's only rated 20% duty at 140 A gives you 2 minutes of welding per 10. Compute the real number with the duty cycle calculator at the amperage your metal needs.

Gas commitment. 75/25 MIG is far nicer than flux-core indoors, but the bottle, regulator, and refill are a real line item. Dual-process machines let you start flux-core and add gas when you're committed.

Thin-metal fantasies. Body panels are a different job from the bracket you practice on. Learn the puddle on thicker scrap with MIG or stick. TIG is a later process, after the thickness you actually weld is already inside a machine you own.

The one-line answer

If you weld anything structural: 140 A dual-process minimum, 200 A if the budget breathes. If you only ever touch thin steel and brackets: a 90–140 A MIG covers it. And if your shop runs off a generator, size that side before the first arc at GeneratorSizer.

Worked example: 3/16 inch, which this site calls 188 mils

The Handler 140 spec sheet lists single-pass mild steel from 24 gauge to 1/4 inch, a welding range of 25–140 A on 115 V, and a rated output of 90 A at 20 percent duty. That sheet is the machine in this example. The thickness is 188 mils, the calculator’s marker for 3/16 inch.

On the stick tab, enter 188 and choose clean. Half of 188 is 94. The function will not go below 62, and 94 falls in the 3/32 inch band (above 66 and at or under 100). Clean metal selects E7018. The stored 3/32 inch E7018 window is 70–100 A. Both ends sit inside 25–140 A, so the power line prints “120 V machine OK.”

On the MIG tab, 188 is not under 188, so the wire is 0.035 inch rather than 0.030. Amps are round(188 × 0.9 / 5) × 5, which is 170. The printed window is 170–196 A. Wire speed is round(170 × 1.6) = 272, through round(272 × 1.1) = 299 ipm. Power prints “220 V machine,” because 188 mils is the function’s cutoff. 170 A is above the Handler 140’s 140 A top, so that MIG window is not a 115 V Handler job.

On the duty tab, enter the Handler rating: 90 A at 20 percent, and set the actual amps to 100, the top of the 3/32 inch E7018 window. Duty is 20 × (90 / 100)² = 16.2, which the tab rounds to 16 percent. Weld time is 1.6 minutes of the 10-minute cycle, and cool-down is 8.4 minutes.

A common mistake

Reading “140 amp” as the amperage you can hold. The same spec sheet that lists a 25–140 A range rates the machine at 90 A and 20 percent. The stick window for 188 mils of clean steel fits inside 25–140 A. The MIG window for that thickness does not. Buying the nameplate and then discovering the MIG tab wanted 170 A is the mistake.

Frequently asked questions

What does the calculator print for 188 mils of clean steel?

188 mils is this site’s 3/16 inch marker. The stick tab prints 3/32 inch E7018 at 70–100 A and a 120 V machine OK, because 70–100 sits inside the Handler 140’s 25–140 A range. The MIG tab prints 0.035 inch wire at 170–196 A and a 220 V machine. 170 A is above that 140 A top.

What duty cycle does the Handler 140 publish?

The Handler 140 spec sheet lists a welding range of 25–140 A on 115 V and a rated output of 90 A at 20 percent duty. On this site’s duty tab, 90 A at 20 percent, while welding at 100 A, prints 16 percent: 1.6 minutes on and 8.4 minutes off in a 10-minute cycle.
