MIG vs TIG vs Flux Core

This page picks the wire and the shielding: solid MIG wire, self-shielded flux core, or gas-shielded flux core. TIG is not a wire in that set. Thickness ranges for MIG, TIG, and stick are on MIG vs TIG vs stick welding. Sheet-metal process choice is on MIG or TIG for sheet metal.

Quick answer: Self-shielded flux core needs no gas bottle, so Miller calls it ideal for welding outdoors or in wind, with excellent penetration on 16 gauge and thicker. MIG runs solid wire under shielding gas, most often 75% argon and 25% CO2, for cleaner welds on thin metal. Lincoln says raising gas flow more than 5 to 10% won't fix a breeze.

What Miller and Lincoln use to choose

The gas bottle is the hard stop. Miller says MIG feeds a solid wire and needs shielding gas from a pressurized bottle, and that gas-shielded flux-cored wire needs external shielding gas too. Self-shielded flux-cored wire does not. Lincoln splits the same family into FCAW-S (self-shielded) and FCAW-G (gas-shielded). Without the bottle, FCAW-G and solid wire are not available.

Wind is the next stop. Miller says solid wire and gas-shielded flux-cored wire are difficult in wind, because losing the gas leaves porosity in the bead, and calls self-shielded wire ideal outdoors or in windy conditions. That portability is why Miller points it at agricultural work far from the shop. Machine class for that work is on what size welder for farm repair. Lincoln says you can raise flow between 5 and 10% for a breeze. Anything more will not solve it and will probably worsen it: the extra gas can bounce off the metal, mix with air, and swirl back into the weld. Lincoln's fixes are a non-flammable barrier (a welding screen, or a weld blanket on a frame) or a flux-cored process that needs no shielding gas.

Thickness is the shop choice, once the air is still and a bottle is there. Miller says solid wire makes a clean-looking weld on material less than 3/16 in. thick down to 24-gauge sheet, and gives a short-circuit example: .030 in. solid wire at 18-19 volts and 160-170 amps with 75% argon and 25% CO2. The same page says the most common solid combination is .035 in. wire with that gas, and that a .035 in. wire on thicker material from a 115-volt circuit may not have the amperage, which raises the chance of cold lap or lack of fusion. Outlet and nameplate limits are on what size welder for a 120V outlet. For thicker metal, or for overhead and vertical-up, Miller says to consider gas-shielded flux-cored wire. Lincoln prefers that wire indoors and says it needs the external gas; without the gas, the bead goes porous. On 16 gauge and thicker, Miller says self-shielded wire also gives excellent penetration. That sentence sits in the outdoor section. It is not a reason to drop a still-air, bottled solid-wire weld on metal that is still under 3/16 in.

For a first process, Lincoln's repair answer leans toward stick or flux-cored when the work is outside, because there is no bottle to carry, and toward MIG when you want a better-looking bead and no slag, with the outdoor limit that comes with the bottle. MIG, Lincoln says there, is best on thin metal. How that lines up with a first machine is on the best beginner welder.

How to use the pick

Set the four controls, read the wire, then open the welding calculator and enter the thickness you measured. The MIG tab's shielding control has two choices: 75/25, and flux-core with no gas. Use 75/25 for solid wire. Use flux-core, no gas, for self-shielded wire. Gas-shielded flux core is not a third choice on that tab, so do not treat the no-gas setting as this wire. Neither wire is "better" in Miller's close: both make sound welds when the settings match the job. Clean the steel first. Flux-cored wire tolerates more rust, mill scale, and oil than solid wire, and Miller still says cleaning is the right preparation.

FAQ

Does self-shielded flux core need a gas bottle?

No. Miller says the weld pool is protected by gas from the burning flux, so there is no external tank. Lincoln calls that FCAW-S and says it is popular outdoors because wind blows external gas away.

Will more shielding gas fix a breeze?

Lincoln says you can raise flow between 5 and 10% for a breeze. Anything more will not solve it and will probably worsen it, including porosity. Block the breeze with a non-flammable screen or a weld blanket, or switch to flux-cored wire that needs no shielding gas.

Which wire is for thin metal?

Miller says solid wire produces a clean-looking weld from less than 3/16 in. down to 24 gauge. The short-circuit example on that page is .030 in. wire at 18-19 volts and 160-170 amps with 75% argon and 25% CO2. Lincoln says MIG is best on thin metal, such as car body panels, and that the bottle limits outdoor work.
